## 4. Discussion

This study presents a weighted gene co-expression network analysis of whole blood transcriptomes from ASD and typically developing controls, identifies ME11 as the primary ASD-associated co-expression module, and evaluates its molecular architecture and cross-disorder relevance through PPI centrality analysis, ADHD validation, and pathway enrichment. Three principal findings emerge. First, ME11 is a robust, FDR-significant co-expression module downregulated in ASD that is enriched for spliceosomal pathways — providing network-level evidence for pre-mRNA processing dysregulation as a feature of ASD blood transcriptomes. Second, ME11 hub genes do not converge with PPI centrality, indicating that the module reflects transcriptional rather than structural co-regulation. Third, ME11 shows no dysregulation in ADHD, establishing it as ASD-specific and demonstrating that the WGCNA-PPI framework can distinguish disorder-specific from shared molecular networks. Together these findings advance the conceptual and analytical foundation for computationally defining AuDHD biology.

### 4.1 ME11 and Spliceosomal Dysregulation in ASD

The identification of ME11 as an ASD-associated co-expression module enriched for mRNA splicing pathways is consistent with, and extends, a substantial body of literature implicating spliceosomal dysfunction in ASD pathophysiology. The landmark study by Voineagu et al. (2011) first demonstrated using WGCNA in postmortem ASD brain that transcriptional and splicing dysregulation are convergent molecular mechanisms across genetically heterogeneous ASD cases, identifying A2BP1/FOX1 — a neuronal splicing factor — as a hub gene in the primary downregulated ASD module. Subsequent work by Parikshak et al. (2016) confirmed genome-wide changes in splicing in ASD brain, and a 2025 Scientific Reports study identified alternative splicing differences in a Spanish ASD cohort, noting enrichment of splicing factor binding sites in ASD-associated alternatively spliced regions. Our finding of FDR-significant Reactome pathway enrichment for mRNA Splicing — Major Pathway (FDR=0.007), Processing of Capped Intron-Containing Pre-mRNA (FDR=0.007), and mRNA Polyadenylation (FDR=0.033) in whole blood co-expression data provides the first network-level evidence that the spliceosomal signal is detectable not only in postmortem brain tissue but also in peripheral blood transcriptomes from toddlers with ASD. This is biologically meaningful: blood-based splicing signatures could serve as accessible biomarkers for ASD-associated transcriptional dysregulation, and the convergence of our blood-based finding with the established brain-based literature strengthens confidence that ME11 captures genuine disease biology rather than tissue-specific noise.

The hub genes of ME11 further support a regulatory rather than structural interpretation of the module. ZCCHC10, a CCHC-type zinc finger RNA-binding protein, is the hub gene most directly consistent with an RNA processing function. RNA-binding proteins of the zinc finger family are central regulators of alternative splicing, mRNA stability, and translational control, and dysregulation of this protein class has been implicated in multiple neurodevelopmental disorders. C1D, a nuclear receptor corepressor, connects the module to transcriptional silencing — consistent with the Cellular Component enrichment for transcription repressor complex (p=4.5×10⁻³) and providing a potential mechanistic link between the transcriptional downregulation of ME11 in ASD and upstream corepressor activity. TMSB4X (Thymosin beta-4) regulates actin dynamics and has established neuroprotective roles, while RAP1B is a RAS-family GTPase involved in integrin signaling and cell adhesion pathways with reported roles in synaptic development. The functional diversity of ME11 hub genes — spanning RNA binding, transcriptional repression, cytoskeletal regulation, and cell signaling — is consistent with a module driven by shared transcriptional regulation rather than a single biological function, supporting the PPI non-convergence finding discussed below.

### 4.2 PPI Non-Convergence: Transcriptional Rather Than Structural Co-Regulation

The absence of significant PPI centrality convergence for ME11 hub genes (Wilcoxon p=0.37) is an informative rather than null finding. Hub genes in co-expression networks can achieve high intramodular connectivity through two mechanistically distinct routes: physical protein interaction (forming a coherent protein complex whose components are coordinately transcribed) or shared transcriptional regulation (whereby genes driven by the same upstream regulatory program co-vary in expression without their protein products necessarily interacting). The PPI convergence test distinguishes between these two scenarios. Our result — that ME11 co-expression hubs are not significantly more central in the STRING PPI network than background ME11 genes — argues against ME11 representing a protein complex and in favor of a shared upstream regulatory driver.

This interpretation is supported by the module's biological profile. A corepressor (C1D) appearing as a hub gene in a downregulated module is consistent with the hypothesis that ME11 is transcriptionally silenced in ASD through coordinated corepressor activity, rather than disrupted as a consequence of protein complex dysfunction. From a translational perspective, this distinction is consequential: a protein-complex-driven module would suggest targeting individual hub protein interactions as a therapeutic strategy, whereas a transcriptionally-driven module points toward identifying and targeting the upstream regulatory factor — potentially a transcription factor or chromatin modifier — responsible for coordinating ME11 expression. Future studies integrating transcription factor binding site analysis or chromatin accessibility data (ATAC-seq) with co-expression network architecture would be well-positioned to identify this upstream regulator.

### 4.3 ME11 is ASD-Specific: Implications for AuDHD Biology

The absence of ME11 dysregulation in the ADHD blood transcriptome (module score t-test p=0.825) is the central finding for the AuDHD framing of this study. This result demonstrates that a co-expression module with robust ASD specificity — surviving FDR correction for module-trait association and showing FDR-significant Reactome pathway enrichment — carries no signal in ADHD blood transcriptomes, despite 50-70% of ASD individuals carrying a concurrent ADHD diagnosis. This finding is consistent with the emerging view from genetic studies that ASD and ADHD, while sharing substantial genetic liability at the population level, are characterized by distinct molecular pathways that can be dissociated at the systems level. A 2024 study applying Genomic Structural Equation Modeling found that genetic pathways unique to ASD, independent of shared ASD-ADHD genetic liability, are enriched for synaptic and neuronal functions — a finding complementary to our transcriptomic result that network-level ASD-specific dysregulation in spliceosomal biology is not reflected in ADHD peripheral blood.

Crucially, the ASD-specificity of ME11 does not imply the absence of shared AuDHD molecular biology — it implies that ME11 is not the shared component. The WGCNA-PPI framework we present here is equally capable of identifying shared modules: modules that are dysregulated in ASD and show parallel dysregulation in ADHD would be strong candidates for AuDHD-relevant biology. The failure to detect such a module in the present study most likely reflects the limitations of the available datasets (discussed below) rather than the absence of shared transcriptional architecture. Indeed, a 2025 study in Molecular Psychiatry identified shared brain connectivity patterns linked to gene expression in children with ASD and/or ADHD using neuroimaging and in silico spatial transcriptomics, suggesting that shared biology does exist at the neural systems level. Identifying its transcriptomic correlate in blood — the tissue available at scale — remains an important open question that larger, better-matched datasets will be positioned to address.

### 4.4 Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant explicit acknowledgment. First, the ASD and ADHD datasets differ in participant age (ASD cohort: toddlers aged 1-4 years; ADHD cohort: adolescents and adults), tissue collection (whole blood leukocytes vs whole blood), and transcriptomic platform (microarray vs RNA-seq). Although these datasets were used for separate analyses — ASD for network construction, ADHD for cross-disorder validation — and were not directly compared, the age mismatch in particular means that the absence of ME11 dysregulation in ADHD could partly reflect developmental stage differences in blood transcriptome composition rather than genuine disorder specificity. Future studies using age-matched cohorts will be essential to confirm the ASD-specificity of ME11.

Second, blood transcriptomics provides a peripheral rather than central nervous system readout. While blood-based co-expression modules have demonstrated biological relevance to brain-based signatures in ASD, peripheral transcriptomes reflect contributions from immune cell composition and systemic physiological state in addition to neural biology. The spliceosomal signal in ME11 may thus reflect immune cell-intrinsic RNA processing changes in ASD rather than — or in addition to — neuronal spliceosomal dysregulation. Cell-type deconvolution analysis was not performed in this study; future work should assess whether ME11 expression is driven by specific blood cell populations.

Third, WGCNA was not feasible on the ADHD dataset (n=76) due to inadequate scale-free topology fit, preventing a module preservation analysis between independently constructed ASD and ADHD networks. This was the originally intended analytical approach; the cross-disorder validation using ADHD differential expression and module scores is a principled but less powerful alternative. Publicly available ADHD transcriptome datasets of sufficient size for WGCNA do not currently exist, representing a gap in the field that future data collection efforts should prioritize.

Fourth, GO enrichment analysis did not identify terms surviving FDR correction, and Reactome enrichment, while identifying four significant pathways, was based on a moderate gene set of 187 characterized ME11 genes. Larger gene sets — achievable through the use of higher-coverage platforms or larger sample sizes — would improve statistical power for enrichment analysis and may reveal additional pathway-level biology.

### 4.5 Future Directions

This study establishes a reproducible, openly available WGCNA-PPI framework for cross-disorder network analysis in AuDHD. Several extensions are prioritized for future work. Access to controlled-access datasets through institutional data use agreements with dbGaP or the NIMH Data Archive would enable analysis of larger, age-matched ASD and ADHD cohorts, increasing statistical power for both network construction and cross-disorder validation. The availability of postmortem brain RNA-seq data for ADHD — currently limited to a single study — represents the most significant data gap in the field; as such datasets accumulate, replication of ME11 (or its brain-tissue equivalent) will be essential. At the mechanistic level, transcription factor binding site enrichment analysis and chromatin accessibility profiling of ME11 genes would help identify the upstream regulatory driver of this corepressor-hub module. Finally, functional validation of ME11 hub genes — particularly TMSB4X and RAP1B — in neurodevelopmental model systems would establish whether their dysregulation has causal relevance to ASD phenotypes or represents a downstream consequence of upstream regulatory changes.
